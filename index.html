The premise

Genome sequencing has revealed an immense reservoir of untapped biosynthetic potential. Most of it has never been expressed, never been seen, and never been put to work. Our job is to change that.

We investigate novel metabolic pathways, enzymes, and bioactive molecules across all kingdoms of life — with the intent to repurpose them against challenges in human health and environmental sustainability.

That means working at two scales at once: dissecting the mechanism of a single enzyme, while asking how resources are balanced across an entire cellular system. The pathway and the physiology are the same problem.

We build bioproduction platforms — and in refining them, we deepen our fundamental understanding of metabolism itself.

Research programs

Four questions,
one continuous loop.

Discover the chemistry. Understand the enzyme. Rebuild the pathway in a host that will actually cooperate. Then use what breaks to learn something new about the cell.

01 / DISCOVERY

Mining the unexpressed genome

Bacterial genomes encode far more biosynthetic machinery than they ever switch on. We use bioinformatic mining, elicitor screening, and comparative metabolomics to wake cryptic pathways up — and to find the molecules nobody has looked for because nobody knew to look.

Genome miningCryptic metabolitesHiTESMetabolomics
02 / MECHANISM

Enzymes that outperform the flask

Biosynthetic enzymes assemble bonds and scaffolds that synthetic routes reach only with difficulty, if at all. We work out what these enzymes actually do to their substrates, how they control selectivity, and what sets the limits of their scope — so that the chemistry becomes predictable rather than serendipitous.

Enzyme mechanismStructure elucidationSelectivityNMR & MS
03 / ENGINEERING

Growth-coupled bioproduction

The fastest way to make a microbe produce a molecule is to make its survival depend on it. We design metabolic couplings that fuse a target pathway to central carbon metabolism, then let selection do the optimization — an approach that took a cephalopod pigment from milligrams to grams per liter.

Metabolic engineeringSelection designHeterologous expressionScale-up
04 / EVOLUTION

Enzymes on demand

We are building high-throughput platforms that pair growth-linked selections with deep mutational scanning, so that an enzyme can be pushed toward a reaction chemistry has always wanted but biology has never needed. New catalysts, chosen rather than designed.

Directed evolutionBiosensorsBiocatalysisSynthetic derivatization

In the news

The octopus
taught us a trick.

On November 3, 2025, Nature Biotechnology published the lab's work — with Leah as lead author — on a scalable microbial route to xanthommatin, the ommochrome pigment behind cephalopod camouflage.

Xanthommatin has fascinated chemists, materials scientists, and the U.S. Navy for decades — and remained almost impossible to obtain. The team engineered a deliberately "sick" bacterium that could only grow if it made the pigment, coupling xanthommatin synthesis to formic acid production that fed cell growth. Survival did the optimization.

The result: 1–3 grams per liter, against roughly 5 milligrams per liter by prior routes — a ~1,000-fold jump that moves xanthommatin from a curiosity to a usable material for photoelectronic devices, thermal coatings, color-changing paints, sunscreens, and sensors.

“It was one of my best days in the lab. When I came in the next morning and realized it worked — and it was producing a lot of pigment — I was thrilled.”

— Leah Bushin, on the experiment that took a year to set up
